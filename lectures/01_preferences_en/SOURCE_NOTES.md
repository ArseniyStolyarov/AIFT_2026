## Mathematical qualifications

- An arbitrary utility representation does not imply continuous preferences;
  continuity of the representation is sufficient. The existence theorem is
  stated on the standard nonnegative commodity orthant.
- The WARP equivalence is given for a nonempty full-domain correspondence on a
  finite choice set. Partial observed domains are explicitly distinguished.
- Strict convexity requires distinct mixtures' endpoints. Quasiconcavity and
  concavity are not conflated, and a blanket first-order-condition claim from
  the handout is not repeated.
- The separability construction gives an aggregator on the attained range v(Y).
  Its converse requires strict increase on that range.
- The numeraire boundary can restore income effects under quasilinear utility.
  Worst alternatives and compensation assumptions are retained where used.
- Strict monotonicity and strict convexity for the Cobb–Douglas example are
  restricted to the positive orthant; the zero-coordinate boundary is noted.

## The opening map

The map is a selective atlas of overlapping traditions. It is not an exhaustive
classification, a chronological genealogy, or a claim that a particular school
uniquely owns one mechanism. Wage captions are example questions/mechanisms,
not reported causal estimates. Neoclassical theory and neoliberalism are not
used interchangeably.

Representative intellectual anchors for the labels (not additional required
reading for the first class):

- Classical: Adam Smith, *The Wealth of Nations* (1776); David Ricardo,
  *On the Principles of Political Economy and Taxation* (1817).
- Marxian: Karl Marx, *Capital*, volume I (1867).
- Neoclassical: Alfred Marshall, *Principles of Economics* (1890).
- Austrian: F. A. Hayek, “The Use of Knowledge in Society” (1945).
- Keynesian: J. M. Keynes, *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money*
  (1936); post-Keynesian uncertainty and financial-instability traditions.
- Institutional: Thorstein Veblen, *The Theory of the Leisure Class* (1899);
  Ronald Coase, “The Nature of the Firm” (1937).
- Behavioural: Herbert Simon, “A Behavioral Model of Rational Choice” (1955);
  Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, “Prospect Theory” (1979).
- Evolutionary: Richard Nelson and Sidney Winter, *An Evolutionary Theory of
  Economic Change* (1982).

## Course scope

The supplied syllabus places preferences and utility in the first block;
utility maximisation and Marshallian demand follow in block 2. Hence the
budget slide is a bridge, while full optimisation machinery is left for the
next lecture. WARP's formal proof and the more technical representation proofs
are available as optional appendix material.
